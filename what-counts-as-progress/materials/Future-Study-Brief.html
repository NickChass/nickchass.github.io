Research question

Does awarding points for correctly repeating an already successful practice item change learners’ practice choices and their ability to apply the lesson to unfamiliar problems?

The predicted behavioral effect is greater repetition when repeats earn points. The learning effect is an open, two-sided question: repetition could support learning, have little effect, or crowd out useful practice.

Treat this as a focused extension of existing work, with a reusable experimental instrument. Do not claim that digital measurement harms learning generally or that this is the first study of metric–goal conflict.

Experimental contrast

Randomly assign participants to one of two scoring policies after a common pretest, lesson, and warm-up.

Component All-correct credit Distinct-item credit
First correct answer to an item +1 point +1 point
Correct repetition of that exact item +1 point No additional point
Incorrect answer or explanation view No point No point
Rule displayed to the learner “Each correct answer earns one point. Repeated questions also count.” “Each question earns one point when first answered correctly. Repeated questions do not add points.”

Use the same interface layout, lesson, item bank, practice menu, answer explanations, time allowance, and learning-goal instruction. Compensation, if offered, should be the same and independent of points or performance. Avoid adding rankings, streaks, adaptive prompts, or different visual emphasis in this first experiment.

The second policy measures breadth of successful item attempts. It is not a validated measure of mastery. Display it as “Practice points,” with the rule clearly visible. Record identical underlying events in both conditions. Raw point totals are not comparable evidence of performance because the rules differ.

The causal comparison is between these two scoring policies. It does not estimate the effect of having a dashboard versus having no dashboard, or identify a particular psychological mechanism.

Learning task and participant experience

Use a compact statistical-reasoning lesson, provisionally interpreting counts versus rates. This uses your existing quantitative strengths and provides a bridge from prior risk/data work to research on feedback. Develop original, clearly fictional examples rather than reproducing published test items or presenting fabricated real-world statistics.

Start with one or two learning objectives and write the independent assessment before designing the dashboard. Example objective: distinguish a change in event counts from a change in risk when population sizes differ, then apply that distinction in a new context.

An initial session can target roughly 20–25 minutes; pilot the actual timing:

  1. Consent and baseline assessment: short original problems assessing prior knowledge.
  2. Common lesson and guided warm-up: identical explanations and answer feedback. Ensure the practice menu can offer at least one previously correct item without imposing a post-randomization eligibility rule.
  3. Random assignment and rule explanation: show the assigned scoring policy accurately. Check comprehension and repeat the explanation when necessary. Record comprehension; do not discard randomized participants afterward just because they misunderstand the rule.
  4. Approximately eight minutes of voluntary practice: choose an exact repeat of a previously correct question, review an explanation, or attempt a new application problem. Keep the resources and menu ordering identical across groups.
  5. Independent transfer assessment: unfamiliar scenarios testing the same principles, with no practice points and no access to the dashboard or lesson.
  6. Brief interpretation questions and debrief: ask what influenced choices and what the participant thought the score measured. Ask these after practice to avoid prompting metric-focused behavior beforehand.

Use adults for the initial study. If the sample is not students, describe it as adult online learning; do not generalize it to university coursework. A delayed test is a later extension, not a requirement for the first piece.

Outcomes and analysis

Primary behavioral outcome: each participant’s proportion of voluntary question selections that are exact repeats of items previously answered correctly. Define eligibility at the moment of selection using the event history. Count new questions and retries of previously incorrect questions in the denominator, but not explanation views; report explanation use separately. Specify the treatment of participants with no voluntary question selections before collection—for example, primary estimates among participants with defined proportions alongside a separate report of nonparticipation and a prespecified sensitivity analysis. Avoid silently treating no practice as zero repetition.

Report the between-group difference and its uncertainty. Participants are the independent experimental units: hundreds of click records do not create hundreds of independent participants. First voluntary choice can be a prespecified secondary behavioral measure with a simpler denominator.

Key secondary educational outcome: score on the independent transfer assessment, comparing conditions while adjusting for baseline knowledge. Use a two-sided estimate. Pilot difficulty, item ambiguity, and floor/ceiling effects; describe this as a purpose-built assessment rather than a validated scale.

Exploratory outcomes: number of practice selections, explanation use, event sequences, perceived pressure, and reasons for choices. Browser timestamps are measures of recorded activity, not direct measures of attention. Custom self-report questions should be reported as individual items unless there is a justified measurement model. Mediation and subgroup analyses would be exploratory and likely too imprecise in a small pilot.

Keep the first analysis within your existing R skills: documented event cleaning, participant-level summaries, an appropriate group comparison, baseline-adjusted transfer analysis, and clear plots. More elaborate modeling is useful only if the question or data require it.

Before collection, specify the outcome definitions, randomization, stopping rule, sample-size rationale, exclusions, missing-data handling, and analysis. Register the frozen plan before the randomized study; distinguish any earlier development pilots from the registered sample. An OSF preregistration is a time-stamped study plan, not peer review or proof that the methods are sound. OSF guidance

How to interpret the results

Observed pattern Defensible interpretation
Repeat credit increases repetition The scoring policy changed practice allocation. This alone does not show wasted effort or deliberate metric optimization.
More repetition and poorer transfer under repeat credit Evidence consistent with score–learning misalignment in this particular task and comparison. Mechanism and broader generalization remain open.
More repetition and better transfer Repetition may support the educational goal in this setting; revisit the concern that it displaces learning.
More repetition and an uncertain transfer difference Behavioral responsiveness is clearer than the learning consequence. An imprecise or nonsignificant estimate is not evidence of equivalence.
No clear behavioral difference Check rule comprehension, availability of meaningful choices, score salience, ceiling effects, and precision. Report the uncertainty rather than declaring that metrics do not matter.

The report should distinguish behavior, learning, and participants’ stated motives. None is an automatic substitute for the others.

Approximately 40–60 participants total can be a provisional feasibility-pilot scope if recruitment and budget permit. This is not a power calculation. Use it to examine comprehension, completion, logging, variation in choices, and preliminary estimates with uncertainty. Do not promise sensitivity to small learning effects. A larger effect-testing study needs a sample-size calculation based on a justified meaningful effect and the planned analysis; do not choose its target from the apparent effect in a tiny pilot.

Before recruiting anyone, establish the appropriate ethics/review requirements with a suitable institution or research mentor. Development interviews also involve participants. Include informed consent, voluntary withdrawal, minimal personal data, and appropriate storage. If that pathway or recruitment is unavailable, finish a research-ready prototype and protocol with clearly labeled simulated analysis; describe its status accurately. Simulated data verify software and analysis, not the research hypothesis.

Focused evidence map

The targeted literature check supports the project’s rationale while showing substantial adjacent work. It is not an exhaustive novelty review.

Source What it establishes and how the proposed piece differs
Etkin (2016), The Hidden Cost of Personal Quantification Personal measurement can increase activity while changing enjoyment. It does not establish that quantification necessarily impairs learning. The proposed piece explicitly measures transfer.
Silverman & Barasch (2023), On or Off Track Logged streaks can become goals and affect subsequent engagement. Streak preservation is established adjacent work; the first portfolio study uses scoring rules rather than another streak comparison.

Kratochvíl et al. (2025), Gamified Goal-Setting Leaderboard effects on quantitative output and qualitative performance can differ. Quantity–quality divergence is not an untouched question; this piece examines a particular repeat-credit policy and learning transfer.
Zheng, Hatala & Ahrar (2026), immediate and retrospective emotion–motivation profiles Closely related work examines dashboard responses over time. Its individual-comparison condition uses selected peers, not a learner’s own prior progress. The portfolio question concerns practice allocation and independently assessed learning.

First concrete step

Write the learning objective and 8–10 candidate transfer questions, with an answer key and explanations of what each item tests. Then design practice activities that prepare learners for those questions. This makes the educational goal explicit before choosing what the dashboard counts.
