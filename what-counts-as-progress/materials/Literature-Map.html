Focused Literature Map: Scores, Goals, and Study Choices

Checked 3 October 2026. A targeted set of primary sources for the proposed scoring-policy experiment, not an exhaustive systematic review or a novelty claim. The broader OULAD measurement literature is discussed in the research report. Interpretive links to the proposed study are hypotheses, not established results about this prototype.

Primary paper and verified access Actual study scope Use in this project and boundary
Etkin, J. (2016). The Hidden Cost of Personal Quantification. Journal of Consumer Research, 42(6), 967–984. Author manuscript; DOI 10.1093/jcr/ucv095 Six experiments involving coloring, walking, and reading; amount, enjoyment, and downstream engagement. Reading-as-work framing attenuated the enjoyment difference. Supports separate measures of activity and experience. It does not establish that all measurement reduces enjoyment or that the proposed scoring rule impairs transfer.
Silverman, J., & Barasch, A. (2023). On or Off Track: How (Broken) Streaks Affect Consumer Decisions. Journal of Consumer Research, 49(6), 1095–1117. Accepted manuscript in University of Delaware repository; DOI 10.1093/jcr/ucac029 Seven studies: fitness, language questions, and games. Study 4 includes an explicit streak-goal measure and subsequent engagement choice. Some studies hold actual behavioral sequences constant while changing their recorded representation. Direct precedent for a displayed record becoming a goal. Intact/broken streaks and continuing an activity are different from correct-repeat scoring and unfamiliar application performance.
Mekler, E. D., Brühlmann, F., Tuch, A. N., & Opwis, K. (2017). Towards understanding the effects of individual gamification elements on intrinsic motivation and performance. Computers in Human Behavior, 71, 525–534. Authors' institutional publication record and abstract; DOI 10.1016/j.chb.2015.08.048 Online image annotation, with points, levels, leaderboards, and control; tag quantity/quality, competence, intrinsic motivation, and causality orientation. Output quantity increased without statistically significant competence or intrinsic-motivation differences. Strong reason to separate behavioral output from subjective motivation. A nonsignificant difference is not equivalence, and tagging is not a learning-transfer task.
Koo, M., & Fishbach, A. (2008). Dynamics of Self-Regulation: How (Un)accomplished Goal Actions Affect Motivation. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 94(2), 183–195. Author-hosted paper; DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.94.2.183 Four studies of completed/remaining actions and commitment certainty. Academic Study 1 uses an exam scenario and stated study-time and effort intentions; other studies involve purchases, social goals, and charity contributions. Progress and commitment are different interpretations of feedback. The proposed policy comparison does not manipulate these constructs or reproduce this framing design.
Ryan, R. M. (1982). Control and information in the intrapersonal sphere: An extension of cognitive evaluation theory. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 43(3), 450–461. Paper in author-associated SDT archive; DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.43.3.450 Experimental informational/controlling feedback, including self-/other-administered feedback and task-/ego-involvement orientations; intrinsic motivation. Motivates alternatives involving feedback meaning. The current all-correct/distinct-item contrast does not independently manipulate control, competence, or autonomy.

Measurement provenance and practical choice

The official Intrinsic Motivation Inventory documentation is an instrument source, not an additional completed study of this scoring policy. It distinguishes interest/enjoyment from competence, effort, value/usefulness, choice, and pressure/tension. It supports considering a documented subscale during instrument development; it does not validate original items merely because they use those construct names.

Protocol v0.1.0-draft instead labels six post-transfer questions as original exploratory items: score-goal endorsement, learning-goal endorsement, interest, enjoyment, competence, and point-related pressure. The goal items establish reported endorsement rather than relative priority or displacement. Keep all six items separate. No automatic index, validated-scale label, or confirmatory mediation claim follows from the literature above. Baseline and T1–T6 assessment items are also original and require piloting.

Bounded contribution

The proposed work compares all-correct versus distinct-item credit, with identical learning content and a previously correct item available before randomization. Its primary outcome is the first voluntary repeat choice; its key educational secondary is independent application performance. This is a focused extension of existing motivation and feedback research. The current project has no participant results, and these sources do not establish that this exact contrast is original.
