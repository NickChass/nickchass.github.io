01

Context & research question

Trinidad and Tobago has experienced a significant spike in violent crime in recent years — yet no localized, real-time crime awareness tool exists for residents. In the US, apps like Citizen have changed how people navigate safety in their cities. Nothing comparable exists for Trinidad.

AlerTT is a real-time crime awareness app built specifically for the Trinidadian context. Before any product decisions could be made, a discovery research study was needed to understand the actual problem space — how people currently access crime information, how that gap affects their behavior, and what they'd need from an app to actually trust and use it.

Primary research questions: How do Trinidadians currently navigate crime awareness? How does the information gap affect daily behavior? What would make a real-time safety app trustworthy and usable in this specific cultural context?

Critically, this study was designed to surface not just feature preferences, but the psychological and behavioral reality of living with crime anxiety in Trinidad — grounding product decisions in real user experience rather than assumption. The research didn't end at a slide deck: its findings became the specification for a working app, now live.

02

Approach

An 18-question mixed-methods survey was designed and deployed across personal networks, WhatsApp groups, Instagram, and Reddit's r/trinidadandtobago community. The instrument was structured in five sections: current behavior, emotional response, app concept reaction, privacy concerns, and demographics.

03

Execution

The survey was built in Google Forms and distributed through a combination of personal WhatsApp groups, Instagram stories, direct family forwards into broader Trinidadian networks, and a post in r/trinidadandtobago. This multi-channel approach was designed to capture demographic diversity — particularly older age groups and respondents from different regions — rather than relying solely on the researcher's immediate peer network.

The instrument combined Likert scales and linear ratings for quantitative analysis with open-ended questions on trust, distrust, and privacy concerns for thematic analysis. Closed questions established behavioral baselines; open questions surfaced the nuanced reasoning behind those behaviors — the kind of insight that would directly shape product decisions.

04

Findings

Finding 01

The information gap is real, felt, and consequential

Only 7 of 83 respondents typically find out about crime incidents near them within minutes. The majority hear the same day (23) or the next day or later (26), and 3 rarely find out at all. The dominant channels — social media, word of mouth, and WhatsApp groups — are slow, unverified, and fragmented. Mean satisfaction with current crime information access was just 4.89 out of 10.

Finding 02

Crime fear is actively constraining how people live

This was the most striking behavioral finding. Crime concern affects daily decisions — routes taken, places visited, travel times — at a mean score of 6.07 out of 10. More concretely: two-thirds of respondents (56 of 83, or 67%) have avoided a place or activity due to fear of crime in the past 6 months, with 31% having done so multiple times. Only 5 respondents (6%) said they had never avoided anything. Crime anxiety isn't abstract — it's shaping real behavior across the country.

Finding 03

Demand for AlerTT is exceptionally strong

Mean likelihood to use AlerTT if it existed: 7.94 out of 10 (median 9). For a concept product with no existing market presence, this is a strong signal. The most-wanted alert types were shooting / gun violence (66 of 83), robbery / theft (62), gang-related activity (57), and police activity / roadblocks (49). The top three are all violent-crime categories — a clear steer for how alerts should be prioritized in the product.

Finding 04 · Most important

Trust and verification are the critical design challenge — not features

The open-ended responses told a consistent and nuanced story. Misinformation and fake news was the single most cited concern. But the trust picture is more complex than "people want verified information." Several distinct trust themes emerged from thematic analysis of the open-ended trust and concern questions:

"I'd trust alerts if the events were real and verifiable. I'd distrust if the app was filled with spam/fake reports."
Respondent · 25–34 · North
"[My distrust would come from] users' ability to view me on the app, as it can be potentially used by criminals to target those who report through the app."
Respondent · 25–34 · West
"I am very distrusting of Police so I would not want to share my location in situations where it is accessible by police."
Respondent · 35–44 · West
"Compilation of videos related to a particular incident would be useful… if no video evidence can be provided, I would generally trust the validity of an alert out of an abundance of caution."
Respondent · 25–34 · North

A significant finding on report handling: 53% of respondents (44 of 83) want both anonymous AND verified options to coexist — not one or the other. This suggests a dual-track verification system rather than a single approach.

Finding 05

Location sharing is conditional — not a dealbreaker

Only 12 of 83 respondents (14%) said they would not share their location at all. The majority are willing under the right conditions: 29 would share only while using the app, 13 are comfortable with always-on, 9 only in certain areas or times, and 20 said it depends on what more information they received. Privacy anxiety is real but addressable through transparent design — not a fundamental barrier to adoption.

05

Impact & recommendations

These findings translated directly into five product decisions — and every one of them is now built into the live app:

  • 01
    Build verification into the core experience — not as an afterthought
    A dual-track system — clearly labeled unverified community reports alongside confirmed reports — directly addresses the trust barrier. Over half of respondents explicitly want both options. Design the information hierarchy around verification status, not recency.
  • 02
    Design for anonymity by default
    Location sharing should be session-only, never persistent. User identity should never be visible to other users. Reporter safety is a genuine concern — one respondent identified the risk of criminals using the app to target people who report incidents. Anonymity is not a nice-to-have; it's a safety feature.
  • 03
    Keep law enforcement at arm's length
    Multiple respondents explicitly expressed distrust of police and concern about government access to their data. Police or government integration — even if well-intentioned — would likely suppress adoption. The app's independence from law enforcement should be a stated design principle and a visible feature of the privacy policy.
  • 04
    Prioritize violent crime alerts in the alert hierarchy
    Shooting, robbery, and gang activity are the top three wanted alert types — all violent crime. The alert priority system should reflect this, ensuring the highest-urgency incidents surface fastest and most prominently.
  • 05
    Solve the speed problem first
    The most urgent user need is timeliness — only 7 of 83 respondents currently hear about incidents within minutes. The app's core value proposition is getting people from same-day awareness to real-time awareness. Every architectural decision should be evaluated against this primary metric.

The broader implication

This research demonstrates that the problem AlerTT is solving is not just a product gap — it's a behavioral one. Crime fear is actively constraining how Trinidadians live: where they go, when they travel, what they do. A well-designed awareness tool doesn't just inform people — it restores agency.

That framing guided every product decision: the measure of success is not engagement metrics or daily active users. It's whether people feel more free to move through their country.

06

From research to a shipped app

The findings didn't stop at recommendations. They became the specification for a working product — and AlerTT is now a live app you can open and use in the browser. It's built with Expo / React Native (deployed to web) on a Supabase backend, with authentication, a real-time alert feed, a map view, dual-track anonymous and verified reporting, a moderation flow, and privacy-first location defaults. Every one of those features maps back to a specific finding from the n=83 study.

07

Reflection

The sample skewed male. 54 of 83 respondents were men. Given that women often experience crime anxiety differently — and that gender differences in fear of crime are well-documented in the literature — a follow-up study with a more balanced gender sample would likely surface different trust and safety concerns worth designing for.

Qualitative interviews would deepen the trust findings. The open-ended responses on trust and distrust were the richest data in the study — but a survey format limits how deep you can go. Follow-up interviews with 6–8 respondents representing different trust profiles would generate more actionable design insight, particularly around the anonymity question.

The police distrust finding deserves its own study. Multiple respondents raised concerns about law enforcement access to their data — some quite strongly. This is a meaningful design constraint that likely varies by region, age, and personal history. Understanding it more precisely would directly shape governance and privacy architecture decisions.

The live app makes usability testing the natural next step. Now that the recommendations are a working product, the next research phase writes itself: moderated usability sessions on the real app to see which trust and verification mechanisms actually land in practice — closing the loop from discovery back into evaluation.
