Fear of Crime in the United States

Who feels unsafe walking alone at night — and why public-safety research should measure perception alongside incident counts.

Data

Output

Role

01

Context & research question

Recorded crime and how safe people feel are related, but they are not the same measure, and they do not always move together. A neighborhood's incident count is a property of places; perceived safety is a property of people — and it shapes whether they walk, take transit, or use a park after dark, whether or not it tracks the latest statistics.

This project builds a public-data companion to my master's thesis on fear of crime, this time in the U.S. and fully reproducible. It asks a question a public agency might actually ask:

Which Americans report being afraid to walk alone at night near home — and do the demographic gaps remain after accounting for the self-reported victimization the data actually capture?

The answer matters for measurement. If who feels unsafe is not simply who was recently victimized, then crime counts alone cannot tell an agency whether residents feel safe — and perceived safety needs to be measured in its own right.

02

Approach

The data source is the U.S. General Social Survey (GSS), NORC's long-running national survey of American adults. The outcome is the GSS FEAR item, asked since 1973: “Is there any area right around here — that is, within a mile — where you would be afraid to walk alone at night?”

Reviewing the documentation surfaced the design's central constraint — and a finding in its own right: the GSS stopped asking its personal-victimization questions (household burglary and robbery) after 1994. Perceived safety and victimization therefore overlap in the GSS only through 1994. So the analysis uses two clearly separated, survey-weighted samples rather than pretending one continuous series exists.

Why the GSS
A defensible public, national instrument
My thesis dataset is private and cannot be shared. The GSS is public, nationally representative, and fully documented — so the whole analysis can be reproduced by anyone from raw data.
Why two samples
Match the question to the data that exist
Prevalence uses the two most recent rounds (2022 + 2024); the victimization-adjusted model uses 1976-1994, the only window where fear and victimization co-occur. Neither is stretched beyond what it can support.
Why survey weights
The GSS is a complex sample
Estimates use NORC's post-stratification weights with design strata and clusters (the survey package), so prevalence and confidence intervals reflect the sampling design, not raw counts.
Why logistic regression
Hold demographics and victimization together
A survey-weighted logistic model estimates each group's fear adjusting for the others and for reported victimization — testing whether the gaps are really about who was victimized.
03

Findings

Finding 01

Fear is common — about 35% of adults — and sharply unequal by group

In the two most recent GSS rounds, women report fear at nearly twice the rate of men (45% vs. 25%). Fear falls steadily as income and education rise, and — reversing the older stereotype — younger adults now report more of it than older adults. Regional differences are small.

Weighted share reporting fear of walking alone at night, by group. GSS 2022 + 2024 (n = 4,541), weight WTSSNRPS; bars are 95% confidence intervals. Source: General Social Survey; author's analysis.
Finding 02

Victims are more afraid — but most afraid people were not recent victims

In the 1976-1994 window, adults who reported a burglary or robbery in the past year were more likely to feel unsafe (53%) than those who did not (40%). But recent victims were only 8% of adults — so most people who felt unsafe had not been victimized. Victimization moves perceived safety; it cannot be the main thing driving it.

Weighted share reporting fear, by self-reported burglary/robbery in the past year. GSS 1976-1994 (n = 17,247), weight WTSSPS; bars are 95% confidence intervals. Source: General Social Survey; author's analysis.
Finding 03 · Most important

The demographic gaps survive adjustment for victimization

In a survey-weighted logistic regression, reported victimization is independently linked to more fear (adjusted odds ratio 1.55) — but adding it to the model barely changes the demographic gaps. The adjusted odds ratio for women is 5.45 with victimization in the model and 5.42 without it; urban residents stand out sharply (2.29 vs. suburban). The people who feel least safe are not simply the people who were most recently victimized.

Model-adjusted predicted probability of fear by group, others held at a common reference profile. GSS 1976-1994 logistic regression (n = 15,691); bars are 95% confidence intervals. Descriptive associations, not causal effects. Source: General Social Survey; author's analysis.
04

What it means

Because who feels unsafe is patterned by who people are and where they live — not just by recent incidents — perceived safety cannot be inferred from crime counts. That has direct implications for how public-safety work is measured and communicated.

Recommendations (following the results, not assumptions)

Measure perceived safety directly and routinely, alongside incident counts. The GSS FEAR item is a validated, low-cost model.

Don't assume crime trends reach every group equally. A citywide drop in recorded crime can coexist with persistently low perceived safety among women, lower-income residents, and city-core neighborhoods.

Evaluate interventions on perception as well as incidents. Lighting, park redesigns, and community programs can change how safe people feel even when incident effects are small — a benefit incident-only evaluations miss.

05

Limitations

Descriptive, not causal. The GSS is a repeated cross-section of self-reports. These are associations describing how groups differ on average — not the effect of changing anyone's circumstances, and not statements about any single neighborhood.

The victimization link is dated. Because the GSS dropped its victimization items after 1994, the adjusted model rests on a 1976-1994 sample. The contemporary demographic pattern is consistent with the same story, but current data cannot re-run that specific test.

Coarse measures. Fear is a single binary item; victimization covers only two crime types; race is three broad categories; place is proxied by a national urbanicity code. The findings are national in scope by design.

06

How it was built

The project is a clean, reproducible R pipeline — data acquisition, cleaning, weighted analysis, and figure/PDF rendering run end-to-end from a single command (Rscript run_all.R), with the environment pinned by renv. Every number in the memo is generated by the pipeline; none are hand-entered. The deliverables are a 5-page policy memo (HTML + PDF), a one-page technical appendix, and three publication-quality figures.
